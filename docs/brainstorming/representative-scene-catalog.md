# Wilson Shipwrecked — Representative Scene Catalog

## Scene 1 — The Good Chair

**Category:** ordinary, habit, preference, quiet

**Setup:**  
Wilson has several places where he could sit: a crude stool he built, a log near the shelter, and a flat rock overlooking the water. The rock is not objectively the most convenient.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson finishes eating and looks briefly at the stool.

He walks past it.

He walks past the log.

He crosses half the camp to the flat rock, brushes sand from exactly the same spot he usually occupies, sits, and watches the sea.

Nothing important happens.

A bird lands nearby. Wilson glances at it. The bird leaves.

Wilson remains there for a while, apparently content.

Eventually he stretches, gets up and resumes his day.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson carries his food to the rock and eats there.
- Rain begins and he stubbornly remains seated for several seconds before reluctantly retreating.
- Something has occupied the rock, so Wilson stands nearby waiting rather than immediately choosing another seat.
- Wilson falls asleep there and wakes after sunset, visibly confused by how late it is.

**Player opportunities:**

- move something onto the rock;
- remove the stool;
- place an interesting object near another sitting location;
- leave the scene completely alone.

**Persistent consequences:**  
The rock can become recognizably “Wilson's spot.” Later disruption of it has meaning despite the rock having no survival importance.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Wilson should feel alive partly because he wastes time doing things simply because he likes them.

## Scene 2 — Breakfast First

**Category:** ordinary, habit, preference

**Setup:**  
Wilson has developed a morning routine involving checking his food storage before doing anything else.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson wakes, scratches himself, exits the shelter and walks toward storage almost automatically.

On the way, he notices ripe fruit lying nearby.

He stops.

He looks at it.

He looks toward the storage.

He continues to the storage anyway.

He opens it, discovers breakfast, eats, and only afterward returns for the fruit.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson impulsively eats the fruit and skips the routine.
- He collects the fruit first but carries it to his usual breakfast location.
- Storage is empty, and the smooth routine turns into irritated searching.
- He discovers spoiled food and spends the morning dealing with that instead.

**Player opportunities:**

- place food along his route;
- remove or relocate the storage;
- put something unexpected inside it.

**Persistent consequences:**  
A repeated morning sequence becomes recognizable enough that the player can predict Wilson before Wilson does anything.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Predictable habits make later surprises much more effective.

## Scene 3 — The Long Way Around

**Category:** ordinary, history, excessive caution

**Setup:**  
Several days earlier Wilson was pinched badly by a crab beside a particular tide pool.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson is carrying wood toward his shelter.

The shortest route passes beside the tide pool.

He approaches it, slows down and looks toward the water.

There is no crab.

Wilson nevertheless takes a ridiculous detour around a palm tree and through soft sand.

He arrives tired, drops the wood and looks back suspiciously at the completely peaceful tide pool.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson throws a pebble toward the pool before approaching.
- He eventually resumes using the route after several uneventful days.
- Another crab appears elsewhere and Wilson becomes suspicious of that entire stretch of beach.
- In urgent circumstances he reluctantly uses the shortcut while visibly tense.

**Player opportunities:**

- move useful objects near the tide pool;
- place something between Wilson and his detour;
- create an incentive to cross the feared area.

**Persistent consequences:**  
An otherwise trivial location becomes emotionally significant because of history.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
The island should accumulate personal geography: safe places, hated places and stupidly feared places.

## Scene 4 — Scientific Method

**Category:** exploration, physical experimentation, knowledge, comedy

**Setup:**  
Wilson has found a sealed metal container and does not know how to open it.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson inspects the lid.

He pulls.

Nothing.

He braces it between his feet and pulls harder.

Nothing.

He tries striking the edge with a piece of wood.

The wood breaks.

Wilson stares at the broken wood, then at the container.

He finds a stone and strikes the lid.

The container dents but remains closed.

Wilson pauses, studies the dent, changes where he strikes and tries again.

The lid finally pops loose and flies past his face.

Wilson celebrates for half a second before cautiously looking inside.

**Possible variations:**

- repeated impacts ruin whatever was inside;
- Wilson abandons the container and returns hours later with another idea;
- the lid unexpectedly opens during one of the “wrong” attempts;
- the container proves empty after all that effort.

**Player opportunities:**

- suggest an experiment;
- place another candidate tool nearby;
- remove the container halfway through;
- put something useful or absurd inside before it is opened, if still unexplored.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Wilson may remember which approaches worked, which failed and whether opening unknown containers has historically been rewarding.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Discovery should look like someone physically figuring out the world rather than selecting an abstract research action.

## Scene 5 — The Perfectly Good Bowling Ball

**Category:** rare object, exploration, unexpected solution, comedy

**Setup:**  
A bowling ball has washed ashore. Wilson needs to crack a hard shell and has previously used heavy impact objects for similar jobs.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson struggles to lift the bowling ball.

He examines the finger holes.

He inserts his fingers incorrectly, nearly drops it on his foot, swears, and tries again.

He eventually raises it above the shell.

He hesitates at the absurdity of what he is doing.

He drops it.

The shell cracks beautifully.

Wilson looks genuinely impressed.

He picks up the bowling ball with newfound respect.

**Possible variations:**

- the ball rolls downhill afterward and damages something else;
- Wilson later prefers it over a sensible tool because it worked spectacularly once;
- he attempts to throw it and immediately regrets the idea;
- it becomes decoration after its practical usefulness is exhausted.

**Player opportunities:**

- move the ball somewhere accessible;
- suggest using it;
- position the target on a slope, potentially creating chaos.

**Persistent consequences:**  
The bowling ball can become a cherished multipurpose “tool” despite being inconvenient.

**Design stress test:** Yes

The joke depends on broad physical compatibility producing a legitimate solution. It raises the question of how many absurd-but-valid interactions the game should tolerate before systemic freedom becomes content-expensive or unreadable.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
The best absurdity is not arbitrary: the stupid solution actually works.

## Scene 6 — Absolutely Not

**Category:** player suggestion, refusal, autonomy

**Setup:**  
Wilson is tired. A mysterious object has appeared near camp. The player suggests that Wilson inspect it.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson notices the object.

He notices the implied suggestion.

He looks at the object again.

Then toward his shelter.

He makes a dismissive gesture and walks to bed.

The mysterious object remains sitting there while Wilson sleeps.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson gets halfway to bed, curiosity wins, and he returns.
- A second suggestion causes visible irritation rather than compliance.
- A second suggestion succeeds, but Wilson approaches reluctantly.
- Wilson waits until morning and investigates on his own terms.

**Player opportunities:**

- insist again;
- move the object closer;
- move it directly into his normal path;
- remove it entirely.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Wilson may later associate the unexplained presence with annoying requests, useful discoveries, or both.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Suggestions remain interesting only if Wilson can meaningfully say no.

## Scene 7 — Fine!

**Category:** player suggestion, insistence, comedy, autonomy

**Setup:**  
The player has twice suggested that Wilson try wearing an unusual hat-like piece of debris. Wilson has refused.

**Visible sequence:**  
The player suggests it a third time.

Wilson stops what he is doing.

He slowly turns toward the object.

He looks outward with visible irritation.

He picks it up.

He puts it on.

It immediately falls over his eyes.

Wilson stands motionless.

He removes it, throws it onto the ground and points angrily at it as though someone else is responsible.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson unexpectedly likes it and keeps wearing it.
- He deliberately wears it incorrectly in apparent mockery.
- He refuses the third suggestion too.
- The object accidentally provides useful rain protection later.

**Player opportunities:**

- insist;
- remove the object after Wilson agrees;
- provide a mirror-like surface;
- leave him alone after provoking him.

**Persistent consequences:**  
The object may become a reminder of player interference, a favorite possession, or something Wilson refuses ever to touch again.

**Design stress test:** Yes

Repeated suggestions need to feel meaningfully different from direct commands without making persistence either useless or an exploit for guaranteed obedience.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
The player should sometimes discover Wilson's personality specifically by failing to control him.

## Scene 8 — The Missing Spoon

**Category:** environmental intervention, divine interpretation, quiet comedy

**Setup:**  
Wilson always leaves a handmade eating utensil beside his cooking area. While he sleeps, the player moves it onto a nearby rock.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson wakes and prepares food.

He reaches automatically for the utensil.

His hand finds nothing.

He looks down.

He checks underneath something.

He spots the utensil several meters away.

Wilson freezes.

He looks at the utensil.

He looks at the place where it was.

He slowly scans the empty island.

He retrieves it.

Before eating, he puts it back in its original place with exaggerated precision.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson assumes he misplaced it and shrugs.
- After repeated incidents he begins hiding it.
- He leaves a small object beside it before sleeping, apparently as a test.
- He abandons the utensil where the player placed it and changes his routine.

**Player opportunities:**

- return it;
- move another object;
- stop interfering;
- arrange several objects unnaturally.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Repeated unexplained rearrangements can gradually become evidence, in Wilson's mind, that something else affects the island.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
The player's existence should emerge through Wilson's interpretation of impossible little events.

## Scene 9 — The Benefactor

**Category:** player intervention, excessive assistance, long-term

**Setup:**  
For many days, whenever Wilson needs a difficult-to-obtain resource, the player conveniently provides one.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson begins a project and discovers he lacks material.

Normally he would search.

Instead, he waits.

He glances around.

Nothing happens.

He waits longer.

He looks outward expectantly.

Still nothing.

Eventually he sighs theatrically and goes to collect the material himself.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson waits so long that another need interrupts the project.
- He performs a small pleading or presenting gesture.
- He stops expecting help after several disappointments.
- The player finally provides the material, reinforcing the expectation.

**Player opportunities:**

- reward the waiting;
- deliberately withhold help;
- provide the wrong resource;
- place the needed resource somewhere that still requires effort.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Wilson's relationship with unexplained assistance may shift toward gratitude, expectation, dependence or resentment.

**Design stress test:** Yes

If assistance can alter Wilson's behavior, the game risks letting the player accidentally train autonomy out of the central character. Conversely, ignoring repeated miracles would make persistence feel fake.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
God Power becomes more interesting when helping Wilson can itself create problems.

## Scene 10 — The Traitorous Fire

**Category:** persistent object history, project, frustration

**Setup:**  
Wilson has repeatedly struggled to light the same fire pit, including one humiliating failure during rain.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson needs a fire.

He approaches the old pit.

Stops.

Looks at it.

He has perfectly usable fuel.

Instead of using the established pit, Wilson walks several steps away and begins arranging a new fire location.

During the work, he occasionally glares at the old one.

**Possible variations:**

- necessity eventually forces him to use the hated pit.
- the old pit lights effortlessly this time, leaving Wilson almost offended.
- Wilson dismantles the old pit out of spite.
- he succeeds with the new fire and celebrates disproportionately.

**Player opportunities:**

- provide dry fuel at the old pit;
- remove materials from the new location;
- extinguish either fire;
- leave the feud alone.

**Persistent consequences:**  
An individual inanimate object acquires a personal reputation unrelated to its present usefulness.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Wilson's history should make the island feel inhabited rather than reset after every interaction.

## Scene 11 — Gerald

**Category:** animal, persistent relationship, running gag

**Setup:**  
The same crab has repeatedly interfered with Wilson's possessions. Wilson has begun recognizing it.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson is eating.

A crab emerges.

Wilson notices it.

He immediately grabs his food and raises it out of reach.

The crab is not approaching the food.

It walks past.

Wilson follows it suspiciously.

The crab continues toward the water.

Wilson slowly lowers his food, embarrassed by his own reaction.

Then the crab abruptly changes direction toward the food.

Wilson screams, “GERALD!” and gives chase.

**Possible variations:**

- Gerald successfully steals something.
- Wilson anticipates the theft and traps the food somewhere inaccessible.
- Wilson becomes so focused on Gerald that another animal takes the food.
- Gerald merely passes by, making Wilson look paranoid.

**Player opportunities:**

- move the food;
- block Gerald's route;
- create a tempting distraction;
- sabotage Wilson's defensive setup.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Gerald can evolve from nuisance into rival, tolerated neighbor, obsession or bizarre companion.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Individual animals become characters through repeated behavior without ever becoming talking NPCs.

## Scene 12 — Victory Lap

**Category:** animal, strong emotion, serious consequence, comedy

**Setup:**  
Wilson has finally prevented Gerald the crab from stealing food after several prior defeats.

**Visible sequence:**  
Gerald approaches the protected food and fails to reach it.

Wilson notices.

He grins.

Gerald tries another angle.

Still impossible.

Wilson celebrates far too enthusiastically, following Gerald along the beach and taunting it with gestures.

Walking backward, Wilson steps into a shallow hole.

He falls hard.

The food tumbles from his hands.

Gerald turns around.

Wilson, still on the ground, watches Gerald calmly take the food.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson is merely bruised.
- he injures his ankle and spends the next day limping.
- Gerald misses the dropped food, preserving Wilson's victory despite the humiliation.
- another animal gets the food first.

**Player opportunities:**

- move the food to safety;
- block Gerald;
- remove an obstacle from Wilson's path;
- do nothing and watch hubris operate.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Wilson may associate the location, Gerald or overconfident chasing with the injury.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Comedy should be allowed to collide with persistent consequences.

## Scene 14 — Roof or Table?

**Category:** competing projects, preference, autonomy

**Setup:**  
Wilson has an unfinished shelter improvement and an unfinished table. Rain is possible but not immediate. The table is clearly less important.

**Visible sequence:**  
Wilson gathers a useful piece of wood.

He walks toward the shelter.

He passes the unfinished table.

Stops.

Backs up.

He tests the table's wobble.

He looks toward the shelter.

Then he uses the wood to fix the table.

Wilson sits at it proudly.

A few moments later distant thunder sounds.

He slowly looks toward the unfinished roof.

**Possible variations:**

- Wilson changes his mind and saves the material for the roof.
- he fixes the table and rain never comes.
- he starts on the table, becomes nervous about the weather and switches midway.
- a player suggestion influences but does not guarantee the choice.

**Player opportunities:**

- suggest either project;
- move the wood;
- provide another resource;
- sabotage the table's stability.

**Persistent consequences:**  
Whichever project loses remains visibly incomplete and may matter later.

**Design stress test:** Yes

The scene requires Wilson to make a comprehensible but potentially bad choice between legitimate motivations. Too much rationality kills personality; too little makes autonomy look arbitrary.

**Why this scene belongs in Wilson Shipwrecked:**  
Wilson's priorities should sometimes make the player argue with the screen.

# Independent Analysis

## Recurring player fantasies

Several fantasies recur across the catalog.

The first is **watching someone live rather than operating them**. Many of the strongest scenes involve no immediate objective: Wilson sits on his rock, checks the fire, arranges shells or notices that Gerald has stopped appearing. These scenes create ownership through observation rather than control.

The second is **knowing Wilson well enough to predict him**. The player recognizes his favorite seat, expects him to protect his food when Gerald appears, knows he hates a particular fire pit and understands why a mushroom frightens him even though Wilson does not.

That creates a particularly valuable form of surprise: not “anything can happen,” but “I know Wilson, so I cannot believe he just did that.”

A third fantasy is **being an irresponsible minor deity**. The player can help, sabotage, experiment with Wilson, answer his apparent attempts at communication or deliberately remain silent. Importantly, intervention is interesting because Wilson interprets it rather than because the player can directly author the outcome.

A fourth is **watching consequences propagate**. A trivial event can become a habit, rivalry, superstition, favorite possession or monument days later.

Finally, there is a strong fantasy of **discovering stories that feel uniquely one's own**. “Wilson built a statue of the crab that kept stealing his food” is considerably more memorable than “the game contains crab statues,” because the former sounds like a history that happened rather than content that was selected.

---

## Recurring Wilson behaviors

Several behaviors seem especially important to making Wilson feel alive.

### Hesitation

Wilson frequently becomes interesting in the moment between noticing something and acting. Looking between the roof and table, mushroom and stomach, aircraft and bush, or shortcut and long route communicates decision-making without exposing internal machinery.

### Returning to unfinished thoughts

Wilson should sometimes walk away from something and later reconsider it. The bottle that remains unopened and the mysterious object investigated only the next morning make his attention feel persistent rather than transactional.

### Disproportionate emotional reactions

His anger at Gerald, hatred of the troublesome fire, attachment to his sitting rock and humiliation after demanding divine help give mundane objects narrative significance.

### Inefficiency

Wilson takes detours, prioritizes decoration over roofing, repeatedly checks safe fires and preserves useless souvenirs. Perfect optimization would destroy much of his personality.

### Physical inspection

Wilson touches, smells, shakes, pokes, hits, braces, carries, drops and cautiously approaches things. The world becomes understandable through visible physical behavior rather than invisible decisions.

### Behavioral adaptation

The most convincing learning is visible later: checking the fire before bed, avoiding a tide pool, changing food storage behavior or approaching an object differently after previous failure.

### Occasional inexplicability

The ball references are valuable precisely because they are rare and unexplained. Similar tiny discontinuities can make Wilson feel like a specific character rather than a completely transparent simulation.

---

## Systems implied by the scenes

At the conceptual level, the scenes repeatedly imply several capabilities.

Wilson needs **persistent recognition of particular things and places**, because “this fire,” “Gerald,” “my rock” and “the place where I fell” frequently matter more than their general categories.

He needs **general expectations about unfamiliar things** so that he can make reasonable assumptions and occasionally be wrong.

He needs **experience to affect future behavior**, including successful discoveries, failures, injuries, preferences, routines and emotional associations.

He needs **multiple simultaneous motivations**. Survival needs, curiosity, ongoing projects, fear, aesthetic preference, habits and player suggestions regularly compete.

He needs **interruptible activity**. Many scenes become interesting because one intention survives interruption and may or may not be resumed later.

He needs **physical experimentation with uncertain outcomes**, followed by some ability to recognize useful relationships.

He needs **projects that persist through ordinary life** rather than temporarily turning him into a construction worker.

The world needs **independent change**: weather, decay, falling fruit, washed-up objects and occasional rare events.

Animals need enough **individual continuity** for one particular creature to become meaningful without requiring every animal to become a deeply simulated character.

Finally, Wilson needs an evolving **interpretation of unexplained intervention**. The important requirement is not that he discover a literal player, but that repeated impossible experiences can alter how he behaves when further impossible things occur.

---

## Potentially unnecessary complexity

Several desirable experiences could easily encourage overengineering.

### Comprehensive physical simulation

The bowling ball opening a shell does not necessarily imply that every combination of every object must support physically emergent results. The fantasy requires enough surprising compatibility to feel systemic, not necessarily exhaustive physical reality.

### Universal individual memory

Gerald and the hated fire are compelling because they matter personally. It does not follow that Wilson needs equally rich historical relationships with every shell, leaf, stick and transient animal.

### Deep ecology

Fruit falling, plants changing, food spoiling and animals appearing make the island feel alive. None of the scenes require a scientifically coherent tropical ecosystem operating beneath them.

### Fully simulated psychology

Hesitation, attachment, fear, frustration and preference are crucial to the visible experience. Reproducing an internally realistic human mind is not.

### Infinite crafting

The statue of Gerald and absurd uses of the bowling ball encourage combinatorial thinking, but the player fantasy is “Wilson surprised me with what he did,” not “every conceivable object arrangement is craftable.”

### Detailed theology

Wilson's interpretation of the unseen presence works because it remains incomplete and behavioral. Explicit doctrine, metaphysics or elaborate belief structures would probably weaken the joke.

### Perfect autobiographical memory

Long-history payoff matters, but forgetting can itself be believable. The desired effect is selective continuity, not perfect archival recall of everything that ever happened.

---

## Product tensions discovered

### Autonomy vs player frustration

Wilson refusing the player is fundamental. Wilson repeatedly ignoring compelling suggestions could become infuriating.

The most interesting space appears to be one where the player can influence Wilson substantially without ever becoming certain that influence equals control.

### Systemic freedom vs content cost

The bowling ball scene is excellent precisely because an absurd interaction behaves logically. Supporting that quality broadly could create enormous animation, interaction and testing demands.

The product probably benefits from creating the *impression* of generous physical possibility without promising universal combinatorial freedom.

### Persistence vs accumulated nonsense

Personal history creates many of the best scenes. Over long runs, however, Wilson could accumulate so many preferences, fears, grudges, routines and associations that his behavior becomes incomprehensible.

A Wilson shaped by history must still remain recognizably Wilson.

### Player power vs story integrity

Moving the spoon produces excellent ambiguity. Teleporting every dangerous object away whenever Wilson approaches it could trivialize the world.

The stronger player intervention becomes, the easier it is for the player to stop watching Wilson and begin arranging outcomes.

### Directed drama vs autonomous disruption

The aircraft scene becomes memorable because Wilson can ruin it. If rare authored events are routinely ruined, however, players may stop caring about them.

Directed scenes therefore expose one of the central product contradictions: they must be structured enough to justify their content cost but fragile enough to belong to the simulation.

### Rare content vs development cost

A wrecked ship appearing once in many hours can be extremely memorable precisely because it is rare. That same rarity means expensive content may be seen by relatively few players.

### Physical freedom vs readability

“Try hitting X with Y” is understandable. As physical possibilities multiply, communicating what the player can suggest without turning the interface into a giant interaction catalog becomes increasingly difficult.

### History vs immediate utility

Wilson avoiding the tide pool, preserving a shell instead of useful equipment or refusing a perfectly functional fire pit makes him believable. Taken too far, history can make him seem incapable of survival.

### Assistance vs learned dependence

If Wilson adapts to player behavior, repeated miracles should matter. But successful adaptation may produce the perverse result that generous players get a less autonomous Wilson.

That tension is particularly valuable because it reinforces the observe → intervene → observe rhythm: restraint is not merely resource optimization; it preserves Wilson's independence.

### Death vs attachment

Persistent history makes Wilson increasingly valuable as a character. That makes emergent death dramatically powerful but also potentially destructive to the player's desire to continue the story.

Resurrection solves continuity mechanically but creates its own tonal and persistence questions.

---

## Missing scene categories

The assignment covers a broad range, but several potentially important Wilson stories emerged indirectly.

### Boredom

Wilson has comfortable food, shelter and no urgent project. What does he do simply because there is nothing worth doing?

Boredom could be an important source of strange projects, games, rituals and experimentation.

### Self-entertainment

Related but distinct: Wilson inventing a game, repeatedly attempting a pointless physical challenge, drawing in sand or trying to beat his own performance at something could generate strong Johnny Castaway-style sequences.

### Losing something personally valuable

The catalog includes material loss, but not enough cases where an emotionally important useless possession disappears. Wilson searching for a favorite shell could test whether attachment remains convincing when it conflicts with survival activity.

### Accidental rediscovery

An object lost many days earlier could reappear after weather, erosion or unrelated work changes the environment. Wilson's reaction could provide a particularly strong persistence payoff.

### Environmental ownership

Over time Wilson may stop merely occupying the island and begin behaving as though parts of it are “his”: cleaning a preferred area, becoming irritated when animals disturb it, maintaining paths or restoring arrangements after storms.

### Self-created tradition

Wilson could repeat something because *he has decided that this is what he does*, rather than because it has practical utility: a sunset ritual, marking another survived day, decorating something after every storm or performing the same ridiculous gesture when a particular event occurs.

### Regret

Wilson makes a choice, later encounters its consequence and visibly recognizes that his earlier decision caused the present problem. This is different from merely learning that something is dangerous.

### Forgiveness

The assignment strongly supports grudges and fear. It says less about Wilson eventually becoming comfortable again with a previously hated object, place or animal. That reversal could make persistent relationships feel less mechanically one-directional.

### Anticipation

Wilson knows something is likely to happen and prepares for it before it occurs: not simply learned routine, but visible expectation. Waiting for a recurring animal, preparing for approaching weather or checking the shore after conditions that previously produced debris could make him seem especially aware of his world.

### False pattern recognition

Wilson experiences two unrelated coincidences and becomes convinced they are connected. This seems particularly appropriate for a character living in a world where genuine invisible intervention also exists.

It creates a useful ambiguity: sometimes Wilson's superstition is ridiculous, and sometimes there really *is* a god moving his spoon.

# Closing Observation

The strongest scenes in this catalog rarely depend on spectacular events.

They depend on **context**.

A crab stealing food is a minor event. Gerald stealing food after fifteen days of rivalry is a story.

A rock moving is trivial. Wilson discovering that his favorite rock has mysteriously moved after weeks of sitting there is a story.

A mushroom causing fear is ordinary game logic. Wilson being terrified of a mushroom because it killed him in a life he cannot remember is a Wilson Shipwrecked story.

This suggests a useful standard for the behavioral test suite:

> A successful system should not merely allow Wilson to perform interesting actions. It should allow an ordinary action today to become funny, strange, touching or disastrous because of what the player watched Wilson do yesterday.

And the strongest target reaction remains:

> “You won't believe what Wilson did.”
